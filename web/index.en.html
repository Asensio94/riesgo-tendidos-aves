Power-line risk for birds

A dynamic map of bird collision and electrocution risk: it crosses the power lines and wind turbines in OpenStreetMap with where and when the birds are (eBird and GBIF records, Movebank GPS tracking) and with the terrain, to decide which pylon, line segment or wind turbine to fix first.

It produces an index per 500 m grid cell, species and month, and a ranking of the pylons, line segments and wind turbines most urgently in need of review. It is recalculated every month. All the code and the output data are public.

1The problem: we know they kill, we don't know which one to fix first

Distribution power lines electrocute the birds that perch on their pylons; conductors and turbine blades strike them in flight. These are the leading causes of non-natural mortality among large raptors and soaring birds in Spain.

Electrocution

Distribution pylons (1-66 kV)

The bird perches on the cross-arm and touches two phases, or a phase and earth. It depends on the pylon design, the type of insulator and whether there are taps or disconnectors. It affects vultures, eagles, storks, kites and Eurasian eagle-owl.

Collision with conductors

Any line, above all the earth wire

Birds with poor manoeuvrability or flying in flocks, at dawn and dusk: common cranes, storks, Eurasian spoonbills, grey herons, great bustards. Worse on hillsides, passes and flyways.

Collision with wind turbines

Blades on ridges and passes

Soaring birds that use the very updraughts that justify the wind farm: griffon vulture, Egyptian vulture, bearded vulture, golden eagle, kites.

What is missing

Spanish Royal Decree 1432/2008 requires dangerous lines inside protection zones to be retrofitted, and there is excellent academic work on specific sites and years. But there is no up-to-date, queryable service that tells a utility, a public authority or an NGO which specific elements to review first, in which month and for which species. Retrofitting is decided from old inventories, from mortality someone happened to come across, or segment by segment once a complaint arrives.

2The idea: risk = exposure × presence × sensitivity

A pylon is dangerous if there are many dangerous pylons at that spot, if sensitive birds are there, and if it is the time of year when they use it. Each of those factors can be estimated from open data and updated automatically.

The index is relative to the region (the 99th percentile equals 100): it ranks, it does not certify mortality. A pylon with an index of 300 does not kill three times more than one with 100; it simply brings together more pylons, more sensitive birds and more months of presence than 99% of the grid cells.

3Data used: all open, none behind a key

The design condition was that anyone could run the project without asking for permission. That holds; the only layer that requires a key (eBird Status & Trends) is ready but not yet loaded.

Species assessed

Strait: griffon vulture, cinereous vulture, Egyptian vulture, Spanish imperial eagle, Bonelli's eagle, golden eagle, short-toed snake eagle, booted eagle, European honey buzzard, black kite and red kite, peregrine falcon, white stork and black stork, common crane and Eurasian eagle-owl. Cantabria swaps the Mediterranean species for bearded vulture, red-billed chough, Eurasian spoonbill and grey heron. Each species carries a weight per mechanism (0-1) and a conservation weight, both adjustable in a single configuration file.

4Results so far

Two pilots with opposite profiles: the Strait, with few pylons but a migration bottleneck and a great deal of GPS data; Cantabria, with 46,000 pylons, no GPS and a mountain avifauna.

Model output

Clear seasonality

Cantabrian pylons peak in February (wintering red kites and arriving storks); the Liébana lines in July; the wind turbines of the Strait during the autumn passage. Retrofitting in the month before the peak is what matters to a utility.

Model output

Projects scored

The same raster scores any polygon in seconds. Applied to the notices from the public consultation observatory, the Somaloma-Las Quemadas wind farm (Campoo) reaches a maximum of 329, peaking in September.

Not yet

No validation against real mortality

The ranking is consistent with what is known about each area, but nobody has gone out to check whether there are carcasses under the top 40 pylons. That is the first action we are asking for (section 6).

5Challenges and problems encountered

Each one changed the code. We set them out because they are the same ones anyone will meet who wants to reproduce or extend the work.

  • GBIF is not built for downloading hundreds of thousands of recordsThe search API becomes extremely slow with deep offsets and returns 503 errors at peak times.Download in annual blocks, most recent first, with three threads, patient retries and a per-block cache so it can resume. Capped at 20,000 records per species and region.
  • Movebank cuts off large downloadsThe published files range from 10 to 700 MB and the server closes the connection halfway through (IncompleteRead); the direct API requires an account.Streaming download filtering by region on the fly, three retries, and files larger than 150 MB are skipped. Movebank is left out of the automated monthly process.
  • No published GPS dataset covers CantabriaThe data on bearded vulture, Egyptian vulture or the vultures of the Cantabrian range exist but are not held in open repositories.The GPS weight became proportional to the number of positions so that the 5 positions found would not alter anything. The real fix is an agreement with whoever holds the data.
  • A single record could send the index soaringA migrating black stork photographed next to a substation with 41 pylons put that cell in first place for the whole of Cantabria.Wider smoothing where there are few records, √n weighting for stacked records, a cap of 20 pylons per cell and a confidence weight per species in the aggregate.
  • The main Overpass server kept failingA list of mirrors with automatic fallback; the response is cached, so in production OSM is queried once a month.
  • The map with 41,000 pylons weighed 37 MB and froze the browserLines go in a single GeoJSON layer and only the 4,000 highest-index pylons are drawn; the rest are in the CSV and the GeoJSON. 7.8 MB.
  • OSM knows little about each pylonOnly 11% of Cantabria's pylons (5% in the Strait) carry any tag for material, insulator or taps. The real hazard of the design, which is what determines electrocution, is known to the utilities and is not public.A default hazard factor of 1.0 and a clear list of which attributes would change the ranking if someone provided them.
  • Records carry observer biasRoads, viewpoints and hotspots concentrate records even where they do not concentrate birds; and no record says at what height the bird was flying.Correction for sampling effort (records of all birds) and GPS where it exists. This is the main limitation and it motivates the next section.
  • There is no open mortality data in SpainWe looked for power line inventories and electrocution registers from the regional governments, NGOs and LIFE projects: aggregate figures are published, not points.What we found is documented (the Basque Government's shapefile of dangerous lines is the only open one) and the statistical calibration is ready for when the data arrive.

6What would improve accuracy, in order

The model already ranks; what is missing is to calibrate it against reality and refine the two weakest layers: the actual presence of birds in rarely visited cells and the actual condition of each pylon.

On the microphones: automatic song identification is done not by eBird but by BirdNET, from the same laboratory (Cornell Lab of Ornithology). It provides presence and phenology with hourly precision, not flight height or collisions: it complements the cameras, it does not replace them.

7What we ask for and what we offer

We ask for

An institutional counterpart to request mortality records and pylon design data from public authorities and utilities; local groups for the field check of the top pylons in the ranking; agreements on the aggregated use of GPS data; and a pilot area in which to install the first acoustic stations.

We offer

Open, documented code with no licence costs; maps and rankings updated every month; new regions in hours (all we need is the bounding box); automatic scoring of the wind farm and power line projects that go out to public consultation, ready for consultation submissions; and GeoTIFF and CSV files that open in QGIS.

Status and next steps

A working prototype (version 0.1, September 2026) with two regions, an automated monthly process and a public website. In progress: the eBird Status & Trends key. Next: field checks of the top pylons and a validation region in the Basque Country.

How it is computed

Everything happens on a grid of 0.005° cells (about 450 × 550 m). For each species s, month m and cell, three indices are computed:

electrocution[s,m] = P[s,m] · w_electro[s] · equivalent_pylons            (pylons ≤ 66 kV only)
line_collision[s,m] = P[s,m] · w_col_lin[s] · km_of_line · (1 + terrain)
turbine_collision[s,m] = P[s,m] · w_col_aer[s] · n_wind_turbines · (1 + ridge)
  1. Download the infrastructure from OpenStreetMapLines classified by voltage: up to 66 kV is distribution (electrocution risk, the scope of RD 1432/2008); above that, collision only. Pylons inherit the voltage of their line. Where no pylons are mapped, 6 per km of distribution line are assumed.
  2. Hazard rating of each pylonA factor of 0.5-2 from the OSM tags: wood 0.5; pin insulator 1.5; strain insulator 1.3; taps, sectioning points and line terminations 1.3; horizontal cross-arms 1.1; portal frames 1.3. With no tags, 1.0. Capped at 20 pylons per cell (more than that is a substation, not a power line).
  3. Monthly presence P by speciesDensity of records of the species divided by the density of records of all birds in the same month (this corrects for the fact that people observe more near roads and viewpoints). Adaptive Gaussian smoothing: 1.5 km with 300 records or more in the month, wider with fewer. Records repeated at the same point, month and year count as √n. Normalised to 0-1 per species.
  4. GPS tracking where it existsIndividual-days per cell and month from the Movebank tracks, blended into P with a weight that grows with the number of positions (up to 50% from 2,000 positions). Five fixes from a passing bird do not move the map; 300,000 do.
  5. TerrainSlope (up to doubling the risk of collision with lines above 25°) and topographic position index at 1.5 km (ridges and passes, where soaring birds cross low and where wind farms are built).
  6. Aggregation and confidenceEach species adds to the total index with a weight of status × confidence, where confidence is min(1, records/500) or grows with the GPS positions: a species with 120 records in the region (common crane in Cantabria) keeps its own layer but does not dominate the ranking.
  7. Scaling and rankingEach index is rescaled so that the region's 99th percentile equals 100, without clipping the maxima. Each pylon takes the electrocution index of its cell multiplied by its hazard rating; each segment, the mean along its route; each wind turbine, that of its cell. The peak month and the three species contributing most are stored.
  8. Publish and repeatInteractive map with layers by month, mechanism and species; report; CSV and GeoJSON of the ranking; 12-band GeoTIFF for QGIS. An automated process recomputes everything on the 2nd of each month with the new records.

Validation

Pending. The ranking has not yet been checked against real mortality: there is no open dataset of electrocuted or collided birds in Spain. The first planned check is against the Basque Government's shapefile of hazardous power lines (region euskadi); once mortality points are available, the species weights will be fitted with a logistic regression.

Limits

  • The index is relative: 100 is the 99th percentile of the cells with infrastructure in the region, and values above 100 are the top 1% and keep their order for the ranking. It is not a probability of mortality: it is meant for prioritising, not for certifying.
  • OSM coverage is incomplete, especially for pylons and voltages.
  • The occurrence data carry observer bias (roads, viewpoints, hotspots) even after the effort correction, and carry no flight height; only some GPS datasets do.

Public data, rules in plain sight and every figure linked to its source. Leads, not verdicts.
